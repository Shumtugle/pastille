The idea

Words you type twenty times a day

"On my way." "Done, everything checked." "Can't talk, will text." An order number, an address with the door code. Typed on a phone keyboard again and again, with the same typos each time.

Pastille keeps six of them on the home screen. A short label on each pill, the whole phrase behind it, as long as it needs to be, with its line breaks intact. One tap copies it; you paste it where you are going anyway.

  • One tap, no window. The phrase goes to the clipboard straight from the home screen. Nothing opens, nothing flickers; the pill floods with its own colour for a moment and returns.
  • Colour by place. Each of the six places has its own colour, and it never moves. The hand learns that the blue pill says "busy" before the eye has read the label.
  • An empty pill asks to be filled. A dashed outline. Tap it and the editor opens on exactly that slot.
  • Leaving saves. Back, Done, Home, a swipe away: whatever was typed in the editor is kept. There is no way to lose six phrases by a slip of the thumb.
  • Every widget is its own. Put two on the home screen, one for work and one for home, and each keeps its own six phrases and its own glass.

On the home screen

A line that grows the way you pull it

It starts as a single line four cells wide: four pills and a round edit button. Pull it wider and more pills fit, up to six; narrower, fewer. Pull it taller and the six pills fold into two rows of three, then three rows of two. The glass behind them dims in four steps, from 10 percent, almost invisible, to 90.

How it thinks

The awkward parts, honestly

Copying without a window

Since Android 10 an app in the background may not read the clipboard, so many widgets open a see-through window for an instant to copy. That window flickers. Writing is a different matter and is allowed: the tap goes straight to the widget's receiver, and nothing appears on screen but the flash of the pill.

The flash must not fight itself

Tap two pills quickly and the first one's fade, a second later, would put out the second one's flash. Every tap carries a number; only the latest is allowed to fade out.

A label is geometry, not taste

Four cells wide, each pill is about two centimetres, and a label gets room for five or six letters. The first build gave it less and cut even "Soon"; now margins and dot are tighter and the face a size smaller. So the label stays short and separate, and the whole phrase lives behind it.

The size depends on who asks

Launchers report a widget's size as a range, portrait and landscape at once. Pastille takes the width of one and the height of the other, which is the upright cell everywhere, and exactly the cell in a launcher that reports one size.

A widget wakes up in a stranger's room

The system often redraws a widget in a fresh process raised for that single frame. Nothing is trusted from memory: phrases, glass and colours are read from storage before every redraw.

Android 13 speaks up

From Android 13 the system shows its own small notice of every copy. Pastille adds nothing on top of it; the flash of the pill is the answer on older phones and the same on newer.

Built

No Android Studio, no Gradle

Every line was written by AI in plain conversation; the owner of the repository described what he wanted and judged the result. Java 8, no libraries, no AndroidX. The toolchain is bare:

aapt2 compile --dir res -o res.zip
aapt2 link -I android.jar --manifest AndroidManifest.xml --java gen -o base.apk res.zip
javac -source 8 -target 8 -bootclasspath android.jar -d cls $(find java gen -name "*.java")
dx --dex --min-sdk-version=26 --output=classes.dex cls
zip -j base.apk classes.dex && zipalign -p 4 base.apk aligned.apk
apksigner sign --ks release.keystore --out pastille-1.2.apk aligned.apk

Two scars worth recording. The resource tools of the build machine could not read the platform jar of API 35, whose resource table is newer than they are; the build compiles against API 34 and still targets 35. And the signing key, first handed over as a bare file, arrived with every byte above 127 replaced by a question mark. Packed in an archive it came through whole, and was checked by its fingerprint before signing.

Access

What it can and cannot reach

Pastille asks for nothing. It has no internet permission, so it cannot send anything anywhere even by mistake. It reads and writes one thing, the clipboard, and only when you tap.

Phrases are kept in the app's own storage on the phone and are left out of system backups. That makes it a poor place for passwords: anything copied sits on the clipboard where other keyboards and apps may see it.
